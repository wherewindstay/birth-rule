Birth Rule

A slow stare at Wolfram code

Enter a birthday

Algorithm

  • Starts every day at 00:00:00 from the binary digits of the birthday as the first row
  • Each second, computes the next row with that minute's rule, then flips each cell with probability p
  • The rule is picked from 24 candidates by hashing the birthday, the date and the minutes counted from midnight. One minute uses one rule
  • p rises from 0.0005 on the hour to 0.02 at the half hour, then falls again until the next hour
  • Each time the page opens, it recomputes up to 86,400 rows from midnight to now. With no stored state, the same birthday and the same time give the same picture

Cellular automata and rule numbers

  • Cells sit in one row, and each cell is 0 or 1
  • Each cell in the next row is set by three cells of the row above: left, self and right
  • Each of the 8 possible three-cell patterns has an output of 0 or 1
  • The 8 outputs, listed from 111 down to 000 and read as a binary number, give the rule number. There are 256 rules, from 0 to 255
  • For example, the output of rule 30 in the table below, 00011110, is 30 in binary

24 rule candidates

  • 30 and 110, each with its left-right mirror and its 0/1-swapped partners: 30, 86, 135, 149 and 110, 124, 137, 193
  • Rules that grow repeating, overlapping triangles from one center cell: 18, 22, 90, 105, 126, 146, 150
  • Rules that settle into a fixed pattern, a 2-step repeat or a one-way drift from one center cell: 4, 50, 108, 178, 184, 232
  • Others: 45, 54 and its 0/1-swapped partner 147
  • The candidates were chosen so the texture changes from minute to minute. They are not a representative sample of the rule classes

Formulas

  • 240 cells, with both ends connected. The screen shows the last 244 rows. Four tick columns are added, so the picture is 244×244 cells and each cell is square
  • Birthday number B = year × 10000 + month × 100 + day. Without a year, the year is 0
  • Key of the day: k = h32(date, B, 0x5CA7F). The date is the integer YYYYMMDD
  • Rule: candidates[h32(k, minutes since midnight, 0xC10C) mod 24]
  • Noise probability: p = 0.0005 + 0.0195 × (1 − cos(2π × minute / 60)) / 2
  • Flip: a cell flips if h32(k, seconds since midnight, cell index) < p × 2³²
  • Color: one of 10 palette colors, PAL[h32(month, day, 0xC0105) mod 10]. The year and the time of day do not affect the color. The same month and day give the same color. The dark background, the bright color of flipped cells and the minute ticks are the same in every color
  • Random date: picks one of the 366 days of a leap year (including February 29) with equal probability. Skips the day already in the input. Fills the picked month and day into the birthday inputs and computes from them. The color is not drawn separately; the formula above sets it
  • h32 is a 32-bit integer hash

  2. Wolfram, S. (1986). Random sequence generation by cellular automata. Advances in Applied Mathematics 7(2), 123–169. doi:10.1016/0196-8858(86)90028-X. Cited for rule 30: the abstract gives the update rule, and the figures on pp. 128–129 show its pattern.
  3. Wolfram, S. (2002). A New Kind of Science. Wolfram Media. Online edition: p. 27, p. 53. Cited for the introduction of rule 30 (p. 27) and the numbering of the 256 rules from 0 to 255 (p. 53).

  5. OEIS Foundation Inc. A051023: middle column of the rule 30 automaton started from a single black cell. Cited as the reference sequence for checking the center column computed on this page.

The name "Wolfram code" is a common label for this numbering. Wolfram's own papers and book write "rule 30".
